Iowa corn / CF revenue

I tested whether spring rain in Iowa could help estimate how much nitrogen fertilizer moves through the state and how much CF Industries reports in second-quarter sales.

For deliveries, I compared measured rainfall with January–June nitrogen volumes. For revenue, I used spring rain forecasts available in the historical archive and compared estimates built from fertilizer prices, weather and CF’s first-quarter shipments.

In both tests, I estimated each year using only earlier years to fit the model.

Methods

I estimated Iowa deliveries with the median of the previous three springs. I compared that baseline with two rain adjustments. One could move the estimate freely. The other kept it within those three springs’ delivery range. I scored all three on the same six years using the absolute error in tons.

Each May 31 revenue estimate starts with CF’s previous Q2 sales, scaled by the year-over-year change in the April nitrogen fertilizer price index. The weather model adds spring rain probabilities from Ames, Cedar Rapids and Sioux City for April 1 through May 20.

Expanding-window validation
For each May 31 forecast, I refit on earlier years whose Q2 results CF had already filed. The training window grows by one year as I move through the chart.
Standardization and ridge regression
I center the spring rain probability on its training average and divide by its training standard deviation. I fit one slope to the log ratio of reported sales to the price estimate. When the rain forecast equals its training average, the weather adjustment is zero. I use a ridge penalty of 1 every year to shrink the fitted slope.
Feature ablation
I compare four estimates on the same years using price alone, price with weather, price with shipments, and price with both. The shipment adjustment multiplies the baseline by the square root of the year-over-year Q1 shipment ratio. Comparing each pair isolates what adding weather did to the forecast.
Mean absolute error
The table averages the size of the miss in dollars. A forecast $10m too high counts the same as one $10m too low.
Training-year influence
In a separate 2025 diagnostic, I removed one earlier year at a time and refit the phosphate-adjusted model. The resulting change in the 2025 estimate shows how much that forecast depended on each training year.

Iowa field corn motivates the question. CF reports company-wide sales across crops and regions. The historical forecasts have archived values, but their exact public release times are uncertain.

II. Spring rain and Iowa nitrogen deliveries

I averaged observed rainfall at three Iowa locations and counted the nitrogen in four fertilizer grades distributed statewide. The January–June totals include deliveries before the rain and sales across crops.

I first estimated deliveries by averaging every earlier spring. I now use the median of the previous three springs. I sort those three delivery volumes and take the middle one. On the same six years (2019–2024), the average miss fell from 159,101 to 92,005 tons, about 67,100 fewer tons per year (42.2%).

Using the last three springs but keeping the mean gave an average miss of 112,972 tons. The original rain model missed by 144,072 tons. I chose the median after comparing these historical results, so its improvement still needs a test on new years.

I also added spring rain to the three-year median, using the same rain adjustment as before. It helped in four of six years, but raised the average miss from 92,005 to 137,610 tons (49.6%). In the wettest spring, 2024, the adjustment pushed the estimate to 908,319 tons. Iowa reported 499,888. I kept the median alone.

In 2021, the dry spring pointed in the right direction, but the model cut 104,091 tons when a cut of 39,563 would have matched deliveries. In 2024, rain was 118 mm above the recent average and exceeded every earlier spring in the sample. Extending the earlier relationship that far added 398,306 tons to an estimate already close to the reported volume.

I tested a rule that keeps the rain-adjusted estimate between the lowest and highest delivery volumes of the previous three springs. Only 2024 changed. The six-year average miss fell to 72,689 tons, compared with 92,005 for the median alone. I chose the rule after seeing 2024, so I would test both on new years.

Conclusion

Spring rain helped estimate Iowa deliveries when I limited the adjustment to the range of the previous three springs. The average miss fell by 19,316 tons compared with the median alone. That gain depends on a rule I chose after seeing the 2024 overshoot. I would keep the median as the benchmark and test both estimates on new years.

For CF’s company-wide revenue, adding Iowa rain forecasts raised the average miss from $147.0 million to $148.7 million in 2022–2026. Prices and first-quarter shipments together gave the smallest miss, $113.4 million. Iowa’s delivery figures cover one state, while CF sells across regions and crops. My next check is the May 31, 2027 revenue estimate, recorded before CF reports its Q2 sales.
